Lesson 0003 · Interleaving

Mixed Retrieval — Name the Tool

The hardest, most valuable rep: deciding which pattern applies, cold, with no chapter heading to tip you off.

Do this after a 2–3 day gap from Lessons 0001–0002. The delay feels like forgetting — that's the point. Reloading a faded memory is what converts fluency (works now) into storage strength (works in the interview three months from now).

Why mix, instead of more blocked reps?

When you drill ten hashing problems in a row (blocked practice), your brain already knows the answer is "hashing" before reading the problem — so you never train the step that actually matters in an interview: looking at a cold problem and choosing the tool. Interleaving forces that choice every time. It feels worse and scores better — a classic desirable difficulty.

So the goal below is not to solve each problem. It's to name the approach and its complexity in under 30 seconds — the 2–3 minute recognition skill from your mission, compressed.

Drill — name the tool before you click

For each one-liner, say the approach out loud, then check. One option is the interview-optimal tool; the rest are plausible traps.

Debrief

Two of these (the sorted-pair one, and top-K) are not best solved by a plain hash map — recognising that boundary is the whole game. The sorted-array pair points straight at your next pattern: two pointers, which buys O(1) space exactly when the input is sorted. Hold that thought.

Interview tell: when you can say "I could hash this in O(n) space, but since the array is sorted, two pointers does it in O(1) — I'll take that tradeoff," you've just demonstrated the Staff-level judgement Google and Meta are scoring.
How did it feel? If you hesitated on 2+ items, that's signal, not failure — tell me which, and I'll generate a fresh mixed set to re-test those specific boundaries. Otherwise you're ready: open Lesson 0004 · Two Pointers, where the interleaving step will start folding hashing back in.
